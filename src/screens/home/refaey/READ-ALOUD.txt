APOLLO LANDINGS: Why every crew landed with the Sun low in the sky.

20 July 1969. Eagle is on its way down, and its computer is steering toward a crater ringed with boulders.
   (replies: "So what did Armstrong do?")

Armstrong took over and flew past them.
NASA wanted visible shadows for moments like this: they help a crew recognise craters and rocks.
   (replies: "Was the light planned?")

Yes. The landing time was chosen for the light.
At touchdown the Sun stood only 10.7° above the horizon.
   (replies: "What was the rule?")

With the Sun too high, shadows wash out and the ground looks flat.
So the target window was roughly 5° to 14°.
   (replies: "Did the numbers hold?")

JPL Horizons agrees: Apollo 12 landed at 5.1°, Apollo 17 at 13.3°, Apollo 16 at 14.8°.
Our engine matches the published angles within 0.1°.
   (replies: "And at the pole?")

Now the twist. At the south-pole sites NASA is studying, the Sun never gets above about 7°, and mostly sits at 1° or 2°.
   (replies: "So what changes?" · "Why is the Sun so low?")

So there you can't choose the Sun's height.
You choose the hours when it clears the ridge in front of you. Finding those hours is this tool’s job.

CHANG'E 4: First landing on the far side of the Moon.

3 January 2019. Chang'e 4 touches down in Von Kármán crater, on the side of the Moon that never turns toward us.
   (replies: "So where is Earth?")

Not in its sky. At touchdown Earth sat 39.2° below the horizon, and in three weeks of Horizons rows it never rose.
   (replies: "So how did it phone home?")

China sent the answer ahead. In May 2018 a relay satellite, Queqiao, flew to a spot beyond the Moon where it sees the far side and Earth at once.
   (replies: "And the messages?")

Every command and every photo took the long way round: lander to Queqiao, Queqiao to Earth.
   (replies: "And the Sun?")

It had sunlight to spare. At touchdown the Sun stood 31.5° up, 4 days after sunrise, with 10 days 19 hours of daylight still to come.
   (replies: "What about the pole?" · "Why can't the far side see Earth?")

At the pole the puzzle flips. Earth is in your sky, but so low that one ridge can hide it for days.
The “Earth up” row on the timeline shows when it is in view.

ATHENA (IM-2): Landed in a crater, on its side. Sunlight could not reach its panels.

6 March 2025. Athena comes down on Mons Mouton, closer to the south pole than any lander before it.
   (replies: "What was it carrying?")

A NASA drill to look for ice and other volatiles, a hopper and small rovers.
All of it ran on sunlight.
   (replies: "How did it land?")

It landed a few hundred metres from its target, in a crater, and ended up on its side.
   (replies: "What was the Sun doing?")

Look at the Sun that day: 2.5° above the horizon.
That low, a crater wall is enough to keep you in shade.
   (replies: "What happened next?")

Sunlight could not reach its solar cells, so the batteries could not recharge.
Intuitive Machines ended the mission less than a day after landing.
   (replies: "What does our map say?" · "What does our map say?")

One honest note: on our terrain map this spot looks sunlit.
Near the lander the map resolves only 10 metres, so smaller shadows can hide from it.
   (replies: "So what should I take from it?" · "What is the best window?")

So read any sunlight forecast, ours included, as the best case for the area.
The last few metres still decide.
